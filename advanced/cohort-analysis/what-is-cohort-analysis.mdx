## The problem with averages

Your overall repeat rate is 25%. Is that good or bad? Getting better or worse? Are customers you acquired last year more loyal than those from two years ago?

Averages hide the answer. Cohort analysis reveals it.

## What is a cohort?

A **cohort** is a group of customers who share a common starting point — typically the month they made their first purchase.

<Frame caption="Returning Customers report: each row is the customers who first ordered in that month.">
  <img src="/images/cohort-heatmap.png" alt="Cohort retention heatmap with one row per first-order month and one column per month since" />
</Frame>

By tracking what each group does *over time*, you can see whether retention is improving, which acquisition periods produced your best customers, and exactly when customers tend to stop buying.

## Key terms

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Cohort">
    A group of customers who placed their **first** order in the same month (or week, or year). A customer belongs to one cohort forever, however many times they order later.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Period (Month 1, Month 2…)">
    Time counted from each cohort's start, not on the calendar. For January's cohort, Month 2 is February; for March's cohort, Month 2 is April. This lets you compare cohorts at the same age.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Retention / return rate">
    The share of a cohort that ordered again in a given period. 15% in Month 2 means 15 of every 100 new customers ordered again the month after their first order.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Lifetime value (LTV)">
    What an average customer from a cohort has spent so far, after refunds. It grows with every repeat order and tells you what a new customer is worth.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Weighted average">
    An average across cohorts where bigger cohorts count for more. A month with 500 new customers shapes it more than a month with 20, so one small, unusual cohort doesn't skew it.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## How to read a cohort heatmap

SaleTides shows cohort data as a color-coded table. This is the **Returning Customers** report with **%** selected:

<Frame caption="The numbered parts are explained below.">
  <img src="/images/cohort-heatmap-annotated.png" alt="Cohort heatmap with five numbered callouts: a cohort row, the Customers column, the Month 3 column, an empty cell on the diagonal edge, and the Weighted Avg row" />
</Frame>

<Steps>
  <Step title="A cohort (row)">
    Everyone whose **first order** was in that month. Here, 51 customers first ordered in January 2026, and you follow that group across the row.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Customers">
    The size of the cohort: how many new customers joined that month. Each percentage in the row is a share of this number.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Month columns">
    How many of the cohort ordered again, counted by months since their first order. **Month 2** is the month after they joined, **Month 3** the one after that, and so on. There's no Month 1 column: that's the month of their first order, so everyone in the cohort ordered then. Compare a column top to bottom to see whether newer customers come back more or less than older ones.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Not reached yet (—)">
    The cohort hasn't been around that long yet, so there's nothing to count. This is why the table has a diagonal edge: the newest cohorts at the bottom have the fewest months filled in.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Weighted Avg">
    Each column averaged across all cohorts, weighted by cohort size, so a month with many new customers counts for more than a quiet one.
  </Step>
</Steps>

Color shows the same thing as the numbers: **darker cells mean more of the cohort came back**. Switch **#** / **%** to see customer counts instead of percentages, and **Month / Week / Year** to group cohorts differently.

<Tip>
  The newest cell in each row (just left of the dashes) is a month still in progress, so it is usually lower. Judge a cohort on its completed months.
</Tip>

## What cohort analysis reveals

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Is my retention improving over time?">
    Compare the Month 3 column across rows. If the newer cohorts (lower down the table) are darker than the older ones above them, your retention is improving: customers acquired recently are coming back more than those acquired earlier.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="When do customers typically stop buying?">
    Look across any row. The biggest drop is usually from the whole cohort (**Customers**) to **Month 2**: most customers who don't come back in the first month after their first order never do. If you can improve that single step, you'll have a disproportionate impact on overall LTV.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Which acquisition period produced my best customers?">
    Find the row with the darkest shading in later columns. That cohort has the best long-term retention — worth investigating what was different about that period (campaign, product launch, promotion, etc.).
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="How much is a new customer actually worth?">
    The **Lifetime Value** report tracks cumulative spend per customer for each cohort. At Month 12 you see what an average customer from that month has spent in their first year: the number that tells you how much you can afford to spend to acquire one.

  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## What good retention looks like

<Note>
  There is no universal benchmark — it varies heavily by product type, price point, and purchase frequency. Use these as rough guides only.
</Note>

| Retention at Month 3 | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Under 10% | Below average — focus on post-purchase engagement |
| 10–20% | Average for most ecommerce stores |
| 20–30% | Good — strong repeat purchase culture |
| 30%+ | Excellent — high customer loyalty |

Subscription businesses should expect much higher retention (80%+ at Month 3 is typical for healthy SaaS).
